Project Scope

This page documents how the raw CareerCompass extract was reduced to the analytical panel used throughout the site. The panel covers one industry and one career pathway, as the project scope requires.

Element Selection
Industry Computer Systems Design and Related Services
NAICS 2022 code 5415
Career pathway Analytics roles: data analyst, data scientist, data engineer, business intelligence, business analyst
Date window 2025-09-10 to 2026-09-10
Final panel size 1,790 postings

Two Changes to the Data

The project has changed its data twice, and both changes shaped this page.

Industry: from pharmaceutical manufacturing to computer systems design. The project was originally scoped to Pharmaceutical and Medicine Manufacturing (NAICS 3254). A volume check in Module 2 found only 39 postings in that industry across the whole original extract, falling to between 2 and 5 once analytics roles were isolated. No four-digit manufacturing code held more than 57 analytics postings, so we moved to NAICS 5415, a standard four-digit code that appears in the assignment’s list of relevant industries.

Source: from the original extract to v2. Module 2 was built on the original extract of 165,386 postings. That extract had two serious defects. Every skill name beginning with a letter from D to Z had been cut off, so tools such as Python and SQL never appeared, and the experience fields were blank on every posting. In Module 3 we rebuilt the panel on the corrected v2 extract, which fixes both. The same filters that produced 287 postings from the original data produce 2,268 from v2 before the date window is applied.

Data Source and Connection

The source is the MET CareerCompass 2026 v2 extract, supplied as 21 Parquet files holding 504,208 postings and 124 variables.

The raw files are not committed to this repository. Each team member downloads them from the course-provided folder into data/MET_CareerCompass_2026_v2/, as described in the README. The processed panel is committed, so the rest of the site renders without the raw extract.

Filtering and cleaning run in PySpark through scripts/build_panel.py, which imports two helper modules, scripts/clean_location.py and scripts/clean_salary.py. The script writes two files: the panel itself, data/processed/career_market_panel.csv, and data/processed/panel_build_stats.json, which records the counts at each step. The numbers on this page are read from those two files, so they update automatically whenever the panel is rebuilt.

Industry Filter

We filtered on NAICS_2022_4, the four-digit 2022 NAICS code attached to each posting (U.S. Census Bureau (2022)), keeping the 19,886 postings coded 5415. The extract also carries two-, three-, five-, and six-digit variants. We used the four-digit field because the assignment specifies four- or six-digit codes, and we take the industry label from NAICS_2022_6_NAME.

Career-Pathway Filter

Filtering on the word “analyst” alone was not workable. Within NAICS 5415 it matched 5,382 postings, including security analysts, QA analysts, and IT support analysts. “Analyst” works as a generic corporate suffix, and including those roles would distort both the skill analysis and the salary distribution.

We therefore defined the role family positively, matching titles that name an analytics role directly rather than excluding unwanted ones after the fact.

Included patterns: data analyst, data scien, business intelligence, bi analyst, data engineer, analytics engineer, business analyst, reporting analyst

Excluded patterns: security, qa, quality assurance, support, trade, exploitation, acceptance testing, network

The inclusion rule kept 2,308 postings, and the exclusion rule removed a further 40.

Date Window

The original extract covered a single quarter, so in Module 2 we kept its full range. The v2 extract is different: its postings reach back as far as 2014, although most are recent. A posting from several years ago says little about the market a job seeker faces today.

We therefore keep the 12 months ending at the latest posting, from 2025-09-10 to 2026-09-10. This removed 179 older postings. It also removed 299 postings whose posted date is blank, since a posting without a date cannot be placed inside the window.

Duplicate Records

All 1,790 postings carry a unique ID, so the panel contains no exact duplicates.

Grouping by title, employer, and posted date does surface repeats: 1,071 postings share all three with at least one other posting, forming 408 groups. In 49 of those groups the locations differ, meaning one opening was advertised in several places. The rest repeat the same location and may be reposts. We kept every posting, since each has its own ID and a job seeker would see each listing separately. Geographic counts are therefore slightly higher than the number of distinct openings.

Salary

601 postings list a salary. Three rules were applied:

  • Values of zero were treated as missing rather than as a salary of zero. These are cases where the source held a structured pay object the provider could not flatten. We also set a floor of $20,000 a year, below which a figure is implausible. Together these removed 10 postings.
  • Postings quoting an hourly rate (69) were converted at 2,080 hours a year, and postings quoting a daily rate (3) at 260 working days, so every figure sits on one annual scale.
  • We report the midpoint of the advertised range, or the lower bound where only one figure is given.

Salary appears on 33% of the panel, which is normal for job-posting data. Salary findings describe the postings that disclose pay rather than the whole market.

Experience Requirements

In the original extract both experience fields were blank on every posting. In v2 the minimum-experience field is filled on 910 postings, but 387 of them list 0 years.

Zero does not reliably mean “no experience required”. In a check on the panel before the date window, 30 percent of postings listing 0 years had senior titles such as Senior, Lead, or Principal, against 52 percent of postings asking for a year or more. Zero appears to stand in for “not stated” about as often as for “entry level”, so we treat it as not stated, the same way we treat a salary of zero. We also set aside 3 postings asking for more than 20 years.

That leaves 520 postings with a usable minimum, or 29% of the panel. Among them the median requirement is 5 years, and the most common is 5 years.

Education Requirements

The extract offers a minimum and a maximum education field. The minimum is filled on 1,073 postings (60%) and the maximum on 1,537 (86%).

In Module 2 we used only the maximum, because the original extract listed “Associate’s degree” as the minimum on 82 percent of postings, which looked like a default rather than a real requirement. In v2 that pattern is gone: 247 postings list it. We now report both fields, since they answer different questions. The minimum shows what a candidate needs to apply, and the maximum shows the highest level an employer mentions.

One caution: 348 postings list a high school diploma as the minimum, which is unusual for data roles and likely reflects standard wording in the posting rather than a genuine requirement.

Work Arrangement

REMOTE_TYPE_NAME needed two fixes. v2 spells on-site two ways, “Onsite” and “On-site”, which we merged. Many postings also leave the field blank, which we grouped with those the source already labels “Unknown”.

That gives 930 postings with no stated arrangement, or 52% of the panel. Shares below are calculated only among the 860 postings that state one, since counting the unknowns would make every arrangement look rarer than it is.

Code 
stated = panel.loc[panel["REMOTE_TYPE_NAME"] != "Unknown", "REMOTE_TYPE_NAME"].value_counts()
rem = stated.rename("Postings").to_frame()
rem["Share of stated"] = (rem["Postings"] / remote_stated * 100).round(1).astype(str) + "%"
rem.index.name = "Work arrangement"
rem

Location

The structured location fields are incomplete. Within the panel, STATE is filled on 1,028 postings, MSA_NAME on 92, and COUNTY_NAME on 39. STATE_NAME is filled on 1,558, but many of those values are “United States”, “Remote”, a two-letter code, or a city fragment such as “Francisco”. LOCATION is filled on every posting, but it is free text with no consistent structure.

We derive one state value per posting in scripts/clean_location.py, checking in order:

  1. Washington DC patterns, such as “District of Columbia” or “Washington, DC”
  2. A full state name inside LOCATION
  3. A two-letter state code inside LOCATION
  4. The STATE column
  5. STATE_NAME, when it holds a valid state name or code
  6. A short list of bare city names, such as Austin or Sunnyvale

Two-letter codes are then converted to full names, so “CA” and “California” count as one state.

The first step fixes an error in our Module 2 cleaner. It took the first state name it found in the text, so “Washington, District of Columbia” was filed as Washington state, and “1331 Pennsylvania Avenue NW, Washington, DC” as Pennsylvania. Every DC posting was affected, which inflated Washington state in the Module 2 geography results. The panel now places 70 postings in DC.

Code 
unspec = panel.loc[panel["state"] == "Unspecified", "LOCATION"].fillna("")
national = int(unspec.str.contains(r"(?i)remote|united states|\bUSA?\b").sum())

The cleaner places 1,413 postings in a state (79%). Of the 377 it cannot place, 323 read as nationwide or remote listings, such as “United States” or “Remote”. These are reported as Unspecified rather than dropped, since a nationwide or remote posting is a real category rather than missing data.

Industry and Occupation

Every posting in the panel is coded NAICS 5415, labeled “Computer Systems Design and Related Services”.

For occupation, the extract offers ONET_NAME, a standard classification. It proved unreliable here: 161 postings (9%) fell outside the four analytics groups it assigns most often, even though their titles are clearly analytics roles, such as “Data Analyst” and “Senior Data Engineer”. Its labels are also hard to read for a student, filing data engineers under “Database Architects” and business analysts under “Management Analysts”.

We therefore added role_family, taken from the job title, which is the same field the career-pathway filter selects on. It places every posting in one of five families:

Code 
rf = panel["role_family"].value_counts().rename("Postings").to_frame()
rf["Share of panel"] = (rf["Postings"] / n * 100).round(1).astype(str) + "%"
rf.index.name = "Role family"
rf

occupation_group, based on ONET, remains in the panel for reference.

Inconsistent Labels

Employer names are not standardized. 40 employers appear under more than one spelling, such as with and without “LLC” or a space, and 41 postings list a web address, often an applicant tracking system, in place of an employer name. We left employer names as supplied, since any merge rule risks combining companies that are genuinely different. Employer counts should therefore be read as approximate.

Coverage varies widely. Title, employer, role family, date, and skills are essentially complete. Salary, experience, and work arrangement are partial, and each is reported against its own denominator rather than the full panel.

Analytical Limitations

Salary reflects disclosing employers only. Pay appears on 33% of postings. Employers that publish salary may differ from those that do not, so salary figures describe the disclosing subset rather than the whole market.

Experience is partial and ambiguous. A usable minimum appears on 29% of postings. Because zero was treated as not stated, the panel likely undercounts genuinely entry-level roles.

Most postings do not state a work arrangement. 52% of postings are Unknown, so remote, hybrid, and on-site shares rest on the 860 postings that state one.

Skills follow the provider’s taxonomy. Skill names are standardized by the data provider, for example “SQL (Programming Language)”, and broad terms such as “Computer Science” sit alongside specific tools. Postings also do not separate required skills from preferred ones.

Role families rely on title keywords. A title that names two roles, such as “Data Analyst / Data Engineer”, is placed in the first matching family.

The window is one year. Keeping only the most recent 12 months makes the panel current, but it means longer-term trends in analytics hiring cannot be observed.

The panel is a sample, not a census. 1,790 postings in one industry over one year are enough to describe patterns, not to estimate the size of the market. Findings are stated as what these postings show rather than as claims about analytics hiring nationally.

Output
