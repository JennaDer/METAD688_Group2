3. Target User

User Profile

The target user is a student, recent graduate, or early-career professional interested in pursuing an analytics-related career within the pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing industry. The user may have foundational experience with data analysis, statistics, business analytics, or related disciplines and some exposure to tools such as Excel, SQL, Python, R, or data visualization platforms. However, they may have limited experience within the pharmaceutical industry and may not yet understand how their existing skills align with available analytics roles.

User Needs

Before applying for analytics positions in pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing, the target user needs a clearer understanding of the types of analytics roles available, the technical and professional skills employers commonly request, typical experience and education requirements, salary expectations, geographic and remote work patterns, and potential skill gaps. This information can help the user evaluate their current qualifications and identify which skills or experiences they should prioritize to become a more competitive candidate.

4. Product Question

Primary Question

How can an aspiring analytics professional evaluate career opportunities in pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing and determine which skills, qualifications, and experiences to prioritize to become a competitive candidate?

Supporting Questions

  1. What analytics roles appear most frequently in pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing?
  2. Which technical and professional skills are most commonly requested?
  3. How do salary, experience, location, and remote-work patterns vary?
  4. Where does the target user have skill gaps?
  5. Which skills should the target user prioritize?

5. Project Rationale

Problem Statement

Students and early-career professionals may not have a clear understanding of the skills and job-market needs required for analytics roles within pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing, which can result in difficulty identifying appropriate roles and determining how well their qualifications align with employer expectations.

Why the Question Matters

Understanding these requirements is important because students and early-career professionals may need more than general analytics knowledge when preparing for a career in a specific industry. Knowing which skills, qualifications, and experience employers are seeking can help job seekers make more informed decisions about their career preparation and identify areas they may need to develop before applying. It also enables them to better communicate their own skill sets to align with market needs.

Value of the Product

The finished product will provide job seekers with the insights and information needed to better position themselves in the job market, assess their skills and identify areas for self-improvement, and better communicate their strengths based on what employers are seeking. By bringing together information about available roles, required skills and qualifications, salary, location, and work arrangements, the product can support users in their job search and help them better understand current market needs.

6. Literature Review

Source 1: Digitalization in pharmaceutical manufacturing

Citation: Gong, S.-J., Kim, H. R., Park, Y.-J., & Kim, J.-E. (2025). Recent trends in continuous manufacturing and digitalization in pharmaceutical process development and manufacturing. Journal of Pharmaceutical Investigation, 55(3), 397-414.

Summary and relevance: This review examines how Pharma 4.0 technologies are changing pharmaceutical production, covering big data, artificial intelligence, digital twins, continuous manufacturing, process analytical technology, and quality by design (Gong et al. 2025). Its argument is that these tools work best when integrated rather than adopted piecemeal. For our project it establishes that analytics in this industry is embedded in the production process itself, not confined to a business reporting function. That shapes what we expect to see in postings, since roles may sit closer to manufacturing operations than a student would assume.

Source 2: Machine learning across pharmaceutical R&D

Citation: Kolluri, S., Lin, J., Liu, R., Zhang, Y., & Zhang, W. (2022). Machine learning and artificial intelligence in pharmaceutical research and development: A review. The AAPS Journal, 24, 1-10.

Summary and relevance: The authors review where machine learning and AI are applied across drug discovery, clinical development, and regulatory work (Kolluri et al. 2022). It matters to us because it shows the demand for analytics skills in this sector is not limited to the factory floor. A company classified under NAICS 3254 may be hiring for clinical data work, commercial forecasting, or process analytics under similar job titles, which is one reason our skill analysis has to look past the title.

Source 3: Regulatory readiness and the smart factory

Citation: Arden, N. S., Fisher, A. C., Tyner, K., Yu, L. X., Lee, S. L., & Kopcha, M. (2021). Industry 4.0 for pharmaceutical manufacturing: Preparing for the smart factories of the future. International Journal of Pharmaceutics, 602, 120554.

Summary and relevance: Written by authors affiliated with the FDA, this paper looks at what adopting Industry 4.0 technologies means for a regulated manufacturing environment, including data integrity and validation (Arden et al. 2021). It gives our project the regulatory dimension that separates pharmaceutical analytics from analytics in less regulated sectors. We expect this to show up in postings as requirements around GMP, validation, documentation, or compliance, alongside the usual technical skills.

Source 4: Reading skill demand from job postings

Citation: Verma, A., Yurov, K. M., Lane, P. L., & Yurova, Y. V. (2019). An investigation of skill requirements for business and data analytics positions: A content analysis of job advertisements. Journal of Education for Business, 94(4), 243-250.

Summary and relevance: This study analyzes job advertisements across business analyst, data analyst, and data scientist roles and finds that some competencies are shared across all of them while others separate the titles (Verma et al. 2019). It supports the method our project uses, which is treating posting text as evidence of what employers actually want. It also warns against assuming a job title means the same thing at every company, which is directly relevant to how we group roles in Module 2.

Source 5: Industry labor-market baseline

Citation: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. (2026). Occupational employment and wage statistics: Pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing (NAICS 325400).

Summary and relevance: The OEWS series reports employment counts and wage distributions by occupation within this specific industry code (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026). We plan to use it as an external check on our posting data. If our Career Match findings on salary or role mix diverge sharply from the BLS picture, that tells us something about our sample rather than about the market, which is a useful guard against overstating what a set of job postings can prove.

7. Expected Dataset Fields

The table below lists the fields we expect to use and what each one supports. Exact column names will be confirmed against the tool in Module 2.

Field or Variable How the Team Expects to Use It
Industry or NAICS code Filter postings to NAICS 3254, Pharmaceutical and Medicine Manufacturing
Job title Identify which analytics roles appear and group them into a role family
Company or employer Check how concentrated hiring is across pharmaceutical manufacturers
Skills Primary input to the skill-gap analysis; rank the tools and competencies employers request
Software or specialized skills Separate tool requirements such as SQL, Python, R, Tableau, and Power BI from general competencies
Certifications Capture regulated-industry requirements such as GMP or validation experience where they appear
Experience (minimum and maximum years) Judge which roles are realistic for an early-career candidate
Education level Record degree expectations, which we expect to run higher in this industry than in analytics generally
Salary (minimum and maximum) Describe compensation ranges and compare them against the BLS baseline
City, state, and metropolitan area Map where roles concentrate, which we expect to cluster around established pharmaceutical hubs
Remote-work status Compare remote, hybrid, and onsite postings
Posting date and duration Confirm the postings are recent and check whether the sample is spread evenly over time

Note on Data Volume

Before we commit to salary and geography comparisons, we will run a posting count on the filtered NAICS 3254 slice in Module 2. If the count is too small to support distribution-level claims, we will treat skills and role mix as our primary findings and report salary and location as descriptive context rather than as conclusions.

8. What We Expect to Explore

We expect to find that analytics roles in pharmaceutical manufacturing ask for a familiar technical core, with SQL, Python, and a visualization tool appearing in most postings, but that the industry adds requirements a general analytics job would not. We anticipate seeing regulated-environment language around GMP, validation, and data integrity, and we expect education requirements to sit higher than in analytics roles overall, with more postings asking for a master’s degree or a life-sciences background. We also expect hiring to concentrate geographically around a small number of established pharmaceutical regions rather than spreading evenly across the country. These are expectations we intend to test against the data, not conclusions, and we are prepared to revise them if the postings show something different.

9. Project Scope and Boundaries

Included

  • Job postings connected to the selected career pathway
  • Pharmaceutical and medicine manufacturing under NAICS 3254
  • Relevant skills, experience, salary, geography, and work arrangements
  • Career-readiness recommendations for the target user

Not Included

  • The entire labor market
  • Careers unrelated to the selected pathway
  • Industries outside the selected industry scope unless needed for context
  • Claims that cannot be supported by the available data
